Keep Quitting Calorie Counting? 7 Ways to Stick With It Without Burning Out (and Track It in Suu)

Published: September 27, 2026 · Furkan Mert Fındıklı · 7 min read

Short answer: Most people quit calorie counting within the first weeks because they try to weigh every bite. What lasts isn't precision but consistency: log roughly, start with just one meal, log right when you eat and look at the weekly average rather than each day. Calorie counting isn't right for everyone.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have or have had an eating disorder, if the numbers make you anxious or you feel guilty after eating, stop counting and talk to a healthcare professional. If you have diabetes or kidney disease, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, set your calorie target with your doctor or a registered dietitian.

Reach out to a professional promptly if you notice:

  • Vomiting after meals, "compensating" with excessive exercise, or an urge to skip meals
  • Unexplained weight loss over a short period
We wrote this guide as the two-person team that builds Suu. We recommend our app, but the advice rests on peer-reviewed studies and the NHS — you can apply the same approach with pen and paper.

Most people's calorie-counting story goes the same way: in week one they weigh everything, in week two they log the whole day at night, in week three they stop. It isn't a discipline problem; the method itself is exhausting. Trying to enter every bite to the gram puts tracking on top of your life instead of inside it.

The good news: the benefit of calorie tracking comes from regular logging, not perfect logging. The approach below is built around making tracking lighter.

Why almost everyone quits in the first weeks

Recording what you eat is one of the most consistent habits in weight management: a review of 22 studies found self-monitoring was consistently linked to weight loss.1 But the same habit wears people down. In a study of 142 participants, people spent an average of 23 minutes a day logging in month one, falling to 15 minutes by month six.2

That study's key finding: what set the people who lost weight apart wasn't the time spent logging but the frequency. Those who lost the most logged in about 2.5 times a day, versus 1.6 for the rest.

The NHS puts average needs at around 2,000 kcal a day for women and 2,500 for men, and stresses that individual needs vary with age, size and activity.3 A single "daily calories" number from the internet won't fit everyone; it's better to set your target from your own data.

Consistent, not perfect: 7 rules

The goal isn't to track more accurately; it's to track more easily.

  • 80% accuracy is enough: Use hand portions instead of a scale — a palm of meat, a fist of rice. Small errors cancel out in the weekly average.
  • Start with one meal: Don't log the whole day; start with your least predictable meal (for most people, dinner or snacks).
  • Log as you eat: By evening you'll forget half of lunch. Short, frequent entries work better than one long batch.
  • Keep repeat meals ready: If breakfast is the same most days, don't type it from scratch every time.
  • Don't forget drinks: Sugary drinks and coffee add-ons are the most overlooked calories of the day.
  • Look at the week, not the day: One high day doesn't ruin anything; the weekly average shows the real picture.
  • Log the bad days, don't skip them: Weddings, holidays, work dinners — logging beats stopping. An empty day is where tracking ends.

If calorie counting isn't for you

Calorie counting is a tool, not an obligation. In a study of 493 college students, those using calorie trackers showed higher eating concern and dietary restraint.4 If the numbers wear you out, these alternatives work too:

  • The plate method: half the plate vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter grains.
  • Track only protein and vegetables: a portion of protein at each meal, several portions of vegetables a day.
  • Use hunger and fullness cues: rate your hunger 1–10 before and after eating.

How accurate is logging by voice or photo?

AI logging removes the most tedious part of tracking — typing — but it's an estimate. Portion size and hidden oil are the biggest sources of error. For the best result, describe the meal briefly ("a bowl of lentil soup, two slices of bread") and review the result once. Since a new habit takes about two months on average to settle in,5 speed matters more than perfect accuracy in the first weeks.

Frequently asked questions

Why is calorie counting so tedious?

Because most people try to weigh every bite and enter it to the gram. In research, time spent logging falls from 23 minutes a day in month one to 15 by month six. Rough logging, ready-made repeat meals and voice logging all lighten the load.

Can I lose weight without counting calories?

Yes. The plate method, focusing on protein and vegetables or following hunger and fullness cues can also create a calorie deficit. Calorie counting is a tool; if it doesn't suit you, it isn't mandatory.

How much time should calorie tracking take each day?

Successful people log more often, not longer. In one study, those who lost the most logged in about 2.5 times a day, while time spent logging fell from 23 to 15 minutes a day over six months.

Do I have to weigh everything?

No. Hand portions such as a palm or a fist are enough for most people, and small errors even out in the weekly average. A scale can be used briefly as a check if progress stalls.

How accurate is calorie logging by voice or photo?

It's an estimate; the biggest errors come from portion size and hidden oil. Describing the meal briefly and reviewing the result improves accuracy. In the first weeks, speed is worth more than perfect accuracy.
