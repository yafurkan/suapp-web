Too Lazy to Work Out? 7 Ways to Start Without Waiting for Motivation (and Track It in Suu)

Published: September 27, 2026 · Furkan Mert Fındıklı · 7 min read

Short answer: Feeling too lazy to work out is usually not a lack of willpower but a high barrier to starting. What works best is shrinking the goal: start with a 10-minute walk a day, decide the time and place in advance, make your progress visible and bring a friend in. In Suu, GPS walks, streaks and a weekly movement leaderboard with friends track this for free.

This article is not medical advice.

It's general information. If you have a heart, lung or joint condition, high blood pressure or diabetes, are pregnant, or have been inactive for a long time, check with your doctor before starting a new exercise routine.

Stop and get medical help if you notice any of these while moving:

  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness
  • Unusual shortness of breath, dizziness or feeling faint
  • Palpitations or an irregular heartbeat

People who feel too lazy to exercise usually aren't lazy; they pay the cost of starting every single time — the clothes, the time, the tiredness and the internal "not today" negotiation. The brain prefers the reward that's close (the couch) over the one that's far away (health). So the fix isn't finding more motivation; it's making starting easier.

The good news: the benefits of movement start at much smaller doses than most people think. According to the World Health Organization, some physical activity is always better than none, and all of it counts.1

How much movement is enough?

The World Health Organization recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week for adults; worldwide, 31% of adults — 1.8 billion people — fall short of that (2022).1 The NHS gives the same target and adds strength exercises on at least two days a week.2

If 150 minutes feels far away, split it: three 10-minute walks a day get you to the same place. At the start, the number of days matters more than the minutes.

In a meta-analysis of 15 cohorts, people taking about 5,800 steps a day had roughly 40% lower mortality risk than those taking about 3,500; the benefit leveled off around 6,000–8,000 steps for adults over 60 and 8,000–10,000 for younger adults.3 If you're starting from zero, your first few thousand steps are the most valuable ones.

7 tactics for when you just don't feel like it

None of these needs strong willpower; they all lower the cost of starting.

  • The two-minute rule: Your goal isn't "work out" — it's "put on your shoes and walk out the door." Most days you'll keep going once you're out; if you don't, you still haven't broken the chain.
  • An if–then plan: "After lunch, I'll walk out of the office door and walk for 10 minutes." Writing down the time and place takes the decision away from your mood in the moment.
  • Remove friction: Lay out your clothes the night before and put your shoes by the door. Every extra step before you start is another chance for an excuse.
  • Pair it with something you like: Save your favorite podcast or show for walks only.
  • Don't go it alone: Meeting a friend or a small weekly competition makes "not today" harder to say.
  • Measure it: In the randomized trials of a review covering 26 studies, people using a step counter walked about 2,500 more steps a day than those who didn't.4
  • Never miss twice: Skipping one day is normal; skipping two is how a new habit starts. In one study a new behavior took a median of 66 days to become automatic.5

Motivation or habit?

Motivation comes and goes; habits stay. For most people, motivation shows up after moving, not before — the "glad I went" feeling after the first 10 minutes. So in the first weeks, count days, not minutes: five 10-minute sessions in a week are a sturdier start than a single 50-minute one.

Even on a long motorcycle ride, a 5-minute walk at every stop does your legs and your focus good. Movement doesn't have to live inside a gym.

At home, at work, on the road: movement with no excuses

If you can't find separate "workout time," build movement into your day:

  • Take the stairs instead of the elevator, at least for two floors
  • Walk during phone calls
  • Get off the bus or train one stop early
  • A 10-minute walk after dinner
  • 10 squats or wall push-ups between episodes

Frequently asked questions

I'm too lazy to work out — how do I get motivated?

Instead of waiting for motivation, make starting easier: cut the goal to a 10-minute walk a day, decide the time and place in advance, lay out your clothes the night before and do it with a friend. Motivation usually arrives after you start moving.

How many minutes of walking a day is enough?

The World Health Organization recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, which works out to 30 minutes on five days. If you're currently inactive, even 10 minutes a day makes a difference — all movement counts.

How many steps a day should I take?

According to a large meta-analysis, the drop in mortality risk levels off around 6,000–8,000 steps for adults over 60 and 8,000–10,000 for younger adults. But the biggest gain comes early: going from about 3,500 to 5,800 steps already lowers risk markedly.

How do I work out at home when I'm lazy?

Instead of carving out workout time, fit movement into your day: squats between episodes, walking during calls, taking the stairs. Keep equipment somewhere visible and keep sessions short to cut the friction.

Does missing one day ruin the habit?

No. A single missed day doesn't break a habit; the real risk is missing two in a row. New behaviors take about two months on average to become automatic, and slips along the way are normal.
