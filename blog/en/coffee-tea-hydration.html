Does Coffee Count as Water Intake? How Tea, Coffee and Other Drinks Add Up (and How Suu Tracks Them)

Published: March 22, 2026 · Updated: September 27, 2026 · Furkan Mert Fındıklı · 7 min read

Short answer: Partly — more than most people think: moderate coffee and tea hydrate you close to water, and the idea that "coffee dehydrates you" doesn't hold for most people. It still doesn't fully replace plain water. Suu counts drinks conservatively: filter coffee with a hydration factor of 0.8, espresso 0.5, black tea 0.9 and herbal tea 0.98.

This article is not medical advice.

Up to 400 mg of caffeine a day is generally considered safe for healthy adults; the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day during pregnancy. If you have a heart rhythm problem, high blood pressure, reflux or an anxiety disorder, talk to your doctor about how much caffeine is right for you.

Cut back and see a doctor if you notice:

  • Palpitations, chest pain or shakiness after caffeine
  • A marked increase in insomnia or anxiety
We wrote this guide as the two-person team that builds Suu. We publish our app's factors openly; they aren't a scientific claim that "coffee dehydrates you" but a cautious calculation that keeps your daily water goal realistic.

"If you drink coffee, have a glass of water too, or you'll get dehydrated" — advice repeated for years. Caffeine's mild diuretic effect is real, but at moderate intakes it isn't strong enough to cancel out the fluid a cup provides.

Below we look first at what research says, then at why and how Suu counts tea, coffee and other drinks.

Does coffee really dehydrate you?

Not in moderation. In a study of 50 men who habitually drank 3–6 cups a day, drinking 4 × 200 ml of coffee a day for three days produced no difference in body water or hydration markers compared with drinking the same amount of water.1 In a trial comparing drinks, milk and an oral rehydration solution were retained longer than water, while coffee, tea and cola weren't clearly different from water.2

So tea and coffee do contribute to your daily fluids. The NHS agrees: water, lower-fat milk and sugar-free drinks, including tea and coffee, all count.3 That still isn't a reason to drop plain water: caffeine, added sugar and milk change the picture.

How Suu counts drinks

Suu counts every drink with a hydration factor: how much of what you drink is added to your water. 200 ml of filter coffee × 0.8 = 160 ml of water. The factors are deliberately cautious, accounting for strong or large coffees, caffeine sensitivity and added sugar so your daily goal stays realistic. The values in the app:

  • 1.0: water, sparkling water
  • 0.95–0.98: herbal tea (0.98), green tea (0.95), coconut water (0.98)
  • 0.9: black tea, milk
  • 0.8–0.85: latte (0.85), filter coffee (0.8), cappuccino (0.8)
  • 0.75: americano, cold brew
  • 0.5–0.6: cola (0.6), espresso (0.5)
  • 0.3–0.4: energy drinks

Sugar is counted separately: every 10 g of sugar in a drink raises your daily water goal by 100 ml. Caffeine doesn't raise it. Alcoholic drinks shouldn't be treated as water; Suu reflects alcohol by raising your daily goal (+250 ml per 10 ml of pure alcohol).

Drip coffee, espresso and iced coffee

A 12 fl oz (355 ml) mug of drip coffee × 0.8 ≈ 285 ml of water. An espresso is small — about 30 ml × 0.5 = 15 ml — so a double espresso adds almost nothing to your water, even though it adds a lot of caffeine. An iced americano or cold brew counts at 0.75–0.8, but a syrup-sweetened iced drink raises your goal for its sugar.

How many cups of coffee is too many?

The European Food Safety Authority considers up to 400 mg of caffeine a day of no safety concern for healthy adults, advises no more than 200 mg in a single dose and no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy.4 That's roughly 3–4 mugs of drip coffee; energy drinks and colas count toward the same total.

The short version

Coffee and tea don't dehydrate you; in moderation they genuinely add to your daily fluids. But they don't fully replace plain water, and caffeine has an upper limit.

The practical approach: enjoy your coffee and tea, still get most of your fluid from water, and keep total caffeine under 400 mg a day.

Frequently asked questions

Does coffee count as water intake?

Partly. Moderate coffee hydrates you close to water and doesn't dehydrate you, but it doesn't fully replace plain water. Suu counts filter coffee with a factor of 0.8: 200 ml of coffee adds 160 ml of water.

Does coffee dehydrate you?

Not in moderation. In a study where people drank four cups a day, hydration markers were the same as on water days. Very high intakes and caffeine-sensitive people may be exceptions.

Does tea count as water?

Largely, yes. Black tea counts at 0.9, green tea at 0.95 and herbal tea at 0.98 in Suu. The NHS lists tea and coffee among the drinks that count toward your daily fluid.

How many cups of coffee a day is safe?

Up to 400 mg of caffeine a day is considered safe for healthy adults — roughly 3–4 mugs of drip coffee. In pregnancy the limit is 200 mg a day. Energy drinks and colas count toward the total.

Does soda count as water?

Partly: cola counts at 0.6 in Suu, and its sugar raises your daily water goal by 100 ml per 10 g. Sparkling water counts fully.

Why does Suu count coffee at 0.8 if research says it hydrates like water?

Research shows moderate coffee hydrates close to water. Suu's factors are a cautious calculation that accounts for strong or large coffees, caffeine sensitivity and added sugar, so your daily water goal stays realistic.
